# Chapter 5 - Affordability And Access

This section addresses the issue of water affordability and access for consumers in order to determine whether desalination or other types of investment will best satisfy the basic need of safe, affordable water. Focus is almost exclusively on domestic water supply, with discussion for other consumer classes at the end of the chapter.

## 5.1 Background

In order to better understand what a desalination/supply plant can and can not achieve, underlying issues facing the urban water sector are analyzed.

While Ghana Water Company Limited (GWCL) coverage is 80%, less than 10% have an in house connection (Rooijen, Splothoff, & Sally, 33rd WED International Conference, 2008). Moreover, access is highly income dependent: within Accra, 90% of high income areas and only 16% of low income zones have a water system connection (in house or neighbourhood) (Ainuson, 2010). Most lower-income residents depend on intermediate suppliers at a cost up to 12 times that charged by the utility and spend considerable percentage of household income on water (SWITCH EU, 2011).

Increasing access and accessibility for all residents is a priority not only for meeting basic human needs, but maintaining a technically and financially viable water utility. This involves covering infrastructure demand with affordable connections and tariffs for all segments of the population. This study aims to better understand the causes of the current state of affairs and identify some specific strategies for increasing water supply access and affordability in the Greater Accra Metropolitan Area (GAMA).

The SWITCH EU project provides reference data and strategic directions with respect to water supply in the metropolitan area of Accra, Ghana (SWITCH EU, 2011). Strategic directions outlined in this project include (ibid):

- Decreasing demand for water;
- Improving GWCL services by:
o Increasing system capacity; o Decreasing physical losses; o Increasing revenues; o Improving access to the utility; o Lower water prices for low income households;
- Ensuring services for people outside the water utility network.

<!-- PDF page 121 -->

Desalination and other strategic investments focused on increasing system capacity (expanding water supply/treatment plants) seem to be the focus of current water sector activities (Minister of Water Resources, Works and Housing, 2012).; there is relatively little attention or investment to other areas, particularly improving access and affordability for the urban poor.

The paper “Urban Water Politics and Water Security in Disadvantaged Communities in Urban Ghana” analyses institutional issues and impacts of private sector participation (Ainuson, 2010). Similarly, Fuest and Haffner provide a comprehensive analysis of private sector involvement and policy/institutional flaws underlying current issues (Fuest & Haffner, 2010).

Based on this research, two areas were determined to be in need of further clarification prior to major new investments such as desalination:

- To understand interactions of the various issues facing water supply and
explain root causes of the problem of water access and affordability;
- To identify more specific policy and investment priorities to improve
water access and affordability.

## 5.2 The Problem of Affordability and Access

With 38.4% of the city population living in slums and unplanned settlements, significant strain is placed on urban infrastructure and meeting basic needs (including water supply) is an administrative and technical challenge (UN Habitat, 2011). Most lower-income households are not able to afford a pipe water connection and face administrative barriers to connecting to the water utility (Ainuson, 2010). Many therefore rely on private vendors to meet potable water requirements at a cost well above the utility rate.

Within the GAMA there are four main water supply sources: the utility water supply managed by Ghana Water Company Limited (GWCL); private water suppliers; community supply; and self-supply (SWITCH EU, 2011). For potable supply, 51.2% of the population has household connection (mostly from GWCL but also some community managed systems; 39% rely on tanker or vender delivery service; and nearly 10% rely on purchases of sachet (plastic bag) water (ibid). The choice of water supply is highly dependent on income level. Generally, high middle income households are connected to the public utility and lower income households use alternate supplies. Moreover, the water supply cost to consumers varies based on the source (see Table 29 below).

Adding to this high cost are issues concerning reliability and safety of supply. Lower income households are often less able to respond financially with supply disruptions (which occur frequently). In a broad sense, these issues are a result of many factors, including poor institutional framework for water management, lack of adequate infrastructure investment, and lack of financial resources (Fuest & Haffner, 2010). - Legacy of Water Privatization (the dominant institutional model for
the urban water sector);
- Barriers to access for the urban poor (institutional and financial

- System inefficiencies (poor performance of existing water systems)

### 5.2.1 Legacy of Water Privatization

The model of private sector water reforms that was imposed on Ghana has prevented low-income urban residents from accessing affordable water. Recent private sector reforms were a mere continuation of a history of top-down, imposed management with respect to urban water supply. A history of mismanagement, artificially low tariffs, and underinvestment led to high loss rates, low coverage, and inefficiencies (Fuest & Haffner, 2010).

By the 1980s, market reforms were seen as a solution to these chronic problems. In 1987, the World Bank became involved in water policy leading to the Water Sector Rehabilitation Project (WSRP 1995-97), which included decentralization of services, removal of subsidies, and tariff increases (World Bank, 1998). The utility GWCL was created as a limited liability company in 1999, to supply water to urban areas of Ghana through externally financed treatment plants and distribution networks (Ainuson, 2010). With urban utilities seen as most lucrative from an investment/economy of scale standpoint, World Bank/IMF reforms included separation of larger urban utilities from small supply systems, and a separate Community Water and Sanitation Agency (CWSA) was created for small

<!-- PDF page 124 -->

communities and rural areas under the direction of the Minister of Water Works and Housing (ibid). Through the 1990s and early 2000s, however, supply problems persisted and worsened in urban areas, largely due to urban sprawl and the inability to keep up with network demand.

More private sector involvement was seen as the solution. In 2006, GWCL entered into a management contract with the private company Aqua Vitens Rand Limited (AVRL) with the goals of improving reliability, ensuring financial sustainability, and providing affordable access to low income residents (Vitens Evides International, 2011). Having failed to meet these expectations, the Government of Ghana did not renew this contract in 2011. Although, extension of supply to the urban poor was one component of the contract, there was marginal improvement in this regard. This may have been due to bias in the particular management model and objectives. Performance of AVRL was evaluated based on ability to convert consumers into billable customers. However, willingness to pay depends on reliability and confidence in the service. Therefore existing customers (mostly higher income) received better service and less rationing. This created a cycle where the poor, having less confidence in the utility service, were unwilling to invest in a connection (Stoler, Fink, Weeks, & al, 2012). While the total number of connections did expand during the AVRL contract, tariffs also increased substantially25 and the issue of high network losses was not resolved (Vitens Evides International, 2011).

The costs of tariff increases were borne disproportionately by the poor. One explanation for tariff increases was the mandate of the regulatory body. During the privatization process, water regulation and tariffs were moved away from government jurisdiction to an independent Public Utility Regulatory Committee (PURC), which was established in 1997 in order to settle water rates, provide regulation and monitoring of GWCL activities. World Bank/IMF loan conditions required PURC to implement tariff adjustments to achieve full cost recovery (Fuest & Haffner, 2010). However, this oversight does not extend beyond GWCL activities and, therefore, oversight is nearly absent for urban poor customers who are reliant on water purchases from unregulated venders. The privatization process that dominated urban water policy in Ghana did little to increase access (during a period of population growth) and actually increased water prices for the poor. This is not necessarily due to privatization itself, but the larger issue of policy making that does not take the interests of the poor into proper consideration. In a thorough analysis, Fuest and Haffner claim decision makers did not adequately consider local capacity and alternate, participatory models. Imposed policies, they claim, were made with lack of institutional coordination and information about poverty reduction (Fuest & Haffner, 2010).

Indeed, the legacy of privatization appears to have created a whole water sector framework unfavorable to low-income consumers. Interests of foreign investors, donor agencies, and higher income customers seem to have been given priority in terms of policy, tariffs, and even technology27. Prior to private sector participation, political interference and subsidized tariffs were used to the benefit of those higher income households which were able to afford a connection (Rooijen, Splothoff, & Sally, Domestic Water Supply in Accra: how physical and social constraints to planning have greater consequences for the poor, 2008).

In the 1990s, for example, tariffs were only $0.10-0.15/m3 and the government did not approve tariff increases. Such financial mismanagement contributed chronic underinvestment and poor utility performance, leading to tariff regulations imposed by donor agencies such as World Bank/IMF. Lower income consumers, however, were left in a largely unregulated environment, with information asymmetry about where to buy water and what to pay (Ainuson, 2010). For example, unregulated private water venders can create artificial scarcity conditions by withholding supply in order to charge higher prices (Rooijen, Splothoff, & Sally, 33rd WED International Conference, 2008).

In all cases, community and consumer priorities seem to have been neglected in water policy. In order to better understand this exclusion, barriers to consumers accessing the utility are analysed.

First, costs of connection are often prohibitive. In order to connect to the GWCL system, consumers must pay GHC80-100/connection, as well as a tank (120GHC) and/or pump (300-800GHC) (SWITCH EU, 2011). While the investment would save money in the long-run, these costs can amount to several months’ income. Second, there is no clear coordinating body responsible for water supply to low- income urban areas. Obtaining a water supply connection presupposes this type of coordination and extension service. While the GWCL mandate covers urban settlements, the Community Water & Sanitation Agency (CWSA) is mandated to supply water to rural areas (Ainuson, 2010). However, slums and peri-urban settlements fall outside of the jurisdiction of both agencies, in urban areas but not considered part of the zoned municipal jurisdiction (ibid). While there have been some successful examples of community managed water supply in these peri- urban areas of Accra, a more systemic approach to alternatives such as public standpipes and community managed supply could improve access and affordability (SWITCH EU, 2011). Also, directing investment and resources to existing GWCL system (central treatment plants and distribution network) may prohibit alternate self-supply in these urban areas, such as rainwater harvesting, standpipes, or household purification systems.

Third, housing policy and unplanned development is also a barrier to water access. Accra continues to experience an influx of migrants, mostly from rural areas, who arrive in unplanned settlements. Proof of ownership is required to obtain a utility connection, but 35% of residents cannot afford their own housing and many slum dwellers are in temporary constructions without title (UN Habitat, 2011). In many of these areas, extension of pipe connections would not be physically possible due to poorly planned construction (Ainuson, 2010). By one estimate, GWCL loses 60% of potential revenue due to these unplanned settlements where many cut distribution lines for their own use or to sell to consumers (Addo, 2010). This results in further planning difficulties since high loss rates lead to a poor understanding of populations served and water use patterns (Rooijen, Splothoff, & Sally, Domestic Water Supply in Accra: how physical and social constraints to planning have greater consequences for the poor, 2008). ### 5.2.3 System Inefficiencies

The lack of a financially-sustainable, reliable water utility creates system underinvestment and poor service, perpetuating affordability and access problems. Currently, sources of funding for the urban water sector projects (country-wide) come mainly from external support/donor agencies which provide about $30 million/year compared to $2 million of government funding and internally generated funds (from water sales) of about $3 million/year (Ghana Water Company Limited). This compares to the $85-100 million required to meet

<!-- PDF page 128 -->

desired coverage levels as guided by Millennium Development Goals, implying a funding gap of at least $50 million/annum (ibid).

In the Accra urban area, physical losses and non-revenue water account for 55% of all water supplied by GWCL, which creates huge revenue losses and low profit margins (Rooijen, Splothoff, & Sally, 33rd WED International Conference, 2008). Physical losses (27% of total water supplied or approximately 35,500,000 m3/year) are mainly due to the poor state of the underground distribution network and poor leakage detection and lack of bulk water metering (SWITCH EU, 2011). Cost estimates for rehabilitation of the distribution system are high at about $123USD/cap (ibid), which translates to est. $225 million for the entire GAMA population28.

Commercial losses such as inaccurate billing and water theft also lead to major financial inefficiency. In Greater Accra, commercial losses amount to about 33% of total water supplied (ibid). Low metering ratio is one cause: 50-70% of all connections are unmetered and there have been inconsistencies/misuse of meter reading and inappropriate implementation of flat usage rates by GWCL staff (Fuest & Haffner, 2010)29. Moreover, many customers do not pay their bills: total monthly GWCL billing is GHC 11.93million while collection is GHC10.85million (Minister of Water Resources, Works and Housing, 2012).

Illegal connections and water theft are difficult to quantify, but are considered to be the main cause of commercial water and total revenue losses. As outlined in the previous section, this is due to unplanned urban settlements, high water costs, and poor distribution coverage. Mitigation measures such as enforcement and monitoring have been implemented, but this is unlikely to be effective unless the root cause of affordable water access is also addressed.

## 5.3 Solution Analysis

A number of solutions/recommendations have been proposed such as those outlined in (SWITCH EU, 2011); also, a number of supply expansion projects have been undertaken, including pro-poor projects and measures (Public Utilities Regulatory Commission, 2008). However, the measures are not extensive enough to meet MDG objectives and underlying management/structural issues outlined above still persist. Large ongoing and planned GWCL capital investment projects address primarily water supply and treatment, including desalination. Though supply expansion is needed, this does not address core problems facing the urban poor or structural issues facing the utility. Moreover, pro-poor projects undertaken by PURC/AVRL were in the pilot stages reaching relatively small numbers of people (Public Utilities Regulatory Commission, 2008).

There remains the need to address the issues more systematically through targeted investments and policies, forming a basis to deal with continued urban growth in the coming years. Also, given limited resources, it is important to prioritize this investment in ways that other literature does not seem to fully do. For example, with water losses over 50% in GAMA, the decision to expand supply before reducing losses could be questioned.

Following the problem diagram (placing lack of pro-poor policies and citizen participation as a core problem in the water sector), the sections below present some community-based approaches to addressing water supply for domestic urban consumers.

Priority approaches outlined below include:

- Integrating small-scale water providers/vendors;
- Expanding public standpipes;
- Rainwater harvesting;
- Integrating urban agriculture and water supply;
- Integrating housing and water policy.

### 5.3.1 Integrating Small-Scale Providers

Indicators show progress towards water coverage for the poor is achieved when consumers have access to piped connections, public standpipes, wells or rain collection within 1 km of their residence (Kjllen & McGranahan, 2006). However, private water venders and small scale providers are the main current supply source for the urban-poor and, therefore, are an important starting point for sector reform. It also must be acknowledged that this water service is an important source of employment for low-income residents operating in the informal sector.

<!-- PDF page 133 -->

Antagonism between government/utility officials and these providers has contributed to high prices and the unorganized, fragmented nature of water services. Rather than maintaining this antagonistic relationship, the recognition of the role these private providers play through their regulation and registration is important (SWITCH EU, 2011). In fact, recent research suggests supressing private vendors can make water access even more difficult and costly unless the utility is able to satisfy all customers, which is unlikely in the foreseeable future (Kjllen & McGranahan, 2006). However, cases where vendors are illegally connecting or interfering with proper utility supply operations need be diminished.

One effective way to integrate vendors is for public agencies to partner with them to ensure technical and health standards are met. Vendors could be licensed (for example with a visual sticker) and failure to comply with regulation could result in revocation (Ainuson, 2010). As part of community information campaigns, consumers could then be informed of appropriate water prices, quality standards, etc. in order to make informed choices. The goal of such programs is public education and expanding information and capacity to consumers and informal service providers. However, this presupposes a body responsible for water extension services to urban poor areas (discussed in the previous section).

Also, market mechanisms and incentive are needed for vendors to comply and participate. Public standpipes are one example of how to reform the role of private vendors. The GWCL Strategic Investment Plan includes plans for 5,880 public standpipes to be built (SWITCH EU, 2011). However, the financial sustainability of this investment will depend on the management arrangement. Partnership with private operators has been shown to be a successful model for public standpipes (Manu & Abrampa, 2006).

### 5.3.2 Public Standpipes

A similar phenomenon of water vendors can be found in other countries, namely Kenya, Nigeria, Sierra Leone, Uganda, and Tanzania and there have been various attempts to improve vendor service in these cases (Ainuson, 2010). Uganda, for example, has shown success in integrating private sector operators to improve water service and affordability. Ninety-one percent of the urban population of Uganda has access to improved water supply (compared to only 67% in the Sub- Sahara Africa region) and the public water utility NWSC is considered one of the most successful in the region (UNDP, 2011). Starting in 2008, the government set clear targets for improved water services in informal urban settlements in the city of Kampala. NWSC used internal funds and capacity to meet the targets which, once met, are reimbursed under an agreement with OBA (Output Based Aid)

<!-- PDF page 134 -->

donor agencies. The most effective supply, they found, were public standpipes with pre-paid meters. This is a way to avoid property ownership requirements and reach large numbers of people (ibid).

Under this arrangement, vendors are permitted to buy bulk discounted tokens/credits and sell them to their customers, who then use them on pre-paid metered standpipes. Rather than regulating vendors out of business, this service forces the water market price down to the utility cost and vendors must adapt. Venders would have a more predicable return on investment and would not have to invest capital in expensive tankers, water pumps, and service equipment which create high operating costs and, therefore, high cost of water (Kjllen & McGranahan, 2006). One problem with this credit meter system was break downs and tampering with meters, but technical training (for utility operators and vendors) as well as community information campaigns are potential ways to address these issues (UNDP, 2011).

Public standpipes are typically installed and owned by the utility. They have an advantage over yard connections or resale from kiosks/private households, since vendors can not hold a monopoly and overcharge33. Prior attempts at providing free water from standpipes have failed due to inability to recover costs (Water Utility Partnership, 2003). Experience shows it is important for those managing the standpipe to have a commercial outlook that promotes cost-recovery and efficiency and private managers or metered standpipes have often been more successful than community managed systems. However, at $2,500USD per unit, water ‘vending machine’ systems are three times and cost of a regular standpipe and up to ten times more than a yard connection. Therefore a token system operated with private vendors is a viable alternative, where an attendant dispenses water (at a profit margin).

In order to lower the price for standpipes, tariffs would need to be adjusted to lower the bulk rate of supply. The current tariff of 0.91GHC for amounts >20m3 is an unnecessary cost to the poor. Selling the water at a rate of 0.70GHC/m3 would then allow for a viable profit margin and selling small volumes could help eliminate the tendency for consumers to use sachet or bottled water.

### 5.3.3 Rainwater Harvesting

Rainwater harvesting is considered an appropriate and underutilized water supply source for GAMA. Average rainfall is about 756 mm/year (SWITCH EU, 2011). A typical system with a large storage tank could provide at least half of consumable water requirements for a family of 5-6. Due to inconsistent rainfall, this could supplement (not replace) water supply, reducing costs and time. Their low operating costs and simplicity make them particularly suitable for households not connected to the utility.

Storage, however, is the main financial barrier to implementation in urban areas. The 9,000 litre storage capacity (needed for withstand the dry season) would require more than one large poly tank, which costs almost 1,000 GHC (or about $500USD) (ibid). Adequate space is also a complication in congested urban areas, thus may not be ideal for many households in high density slum areas. However, as outlined in the next section, the numerous urban spaces currently being used for agriculture provide interesting prospects for potable water supply, including rainwater harvesting which is not limited to rooftop capture and can include groundwater recharge, constructed wetlands, etc. Also, widespread rainwater harvesting of this kind could help alleviate runoff and flooding, a major problem in the city. For consumption of rainwater, sand filtration or other treatment may be required, but natural purification does occur in storage tanks and low-cost methods (eg. sedimentation with chlorination) could be implemented in conjunction with licensed utility operators/vendors and proper community education.

### 5.3.4 Urban Agriculture and Water Supply

Up to 60% of Accra residents engage in some form of backyard/urban gardening, indicating space is available for rainwater capture or other water sources that are currently used for irrigation. This takes place on over 80,000 plots ranging from 0.01-0.02 hectares/farmer within the city and up to 20 hectares in peri-urban areas (Recource Centres on Urban Agriculture and Food Security). Most open green space in the city is used for food production due to high demand for the produce. In fact, 80% of fresh exotic vegetables sold in Accra are grown within the urban area (ibid). Urban farmers already utilize various sources of water and face many similar barriers characteristic of the water sector—lack of land title, lack of institutional coordination, and little organization among growers.

### 5.3.5 Housing Policy

Affordable housing and water access are complementary. In addition to short/medium-term alternatives outlined above, facilitating network connections and allowing tenants to connect to the water utility is essential for long term access. This would require financing mechanisms to overcome the now prohibitive costs of connection. Moreover, expanding this formal infrastructure to temporary, informal settlements is not an option (ibid). Therefore, expanding the distribution network to new/planned settlements should be priority, with other informal settlements served by standpipes or options outlined above.

Annually and estimated 25,000 housing units are needed in the Accra area, while only 5,350 are provided, leaving an accumulated backlog of 300,000 substandard units (UN Habitat, 2011). Therefore, expansion of the distribution network presupposes reform and access to affordable housing. This includes access to the underutilized, vacant land affordable financing mechanisms35. Ensuring all settlements are in proximity to a main road, which is co-constructed with water distribution, is another important way to guarantee water access. Indeed, this is a precondition for installing public standpipes. The Citywide Slum Upgrading and Prevention Strategy estimates the cost of such a housing realignment (including access to roadways, drainage, and water) to be about $30 million by 2020.

The Accra Citywide Slum Upgrading and Prevention Strategy includes a series of policy proposals with costs estimates totalling over $500 million. Many of the concerns addressed, overlap those of the urban agriculture sector, such as land tenure and access to financing. Similarly, the GWCL Strategic Investment Plan includes cost estimates over $800 million. Given the considerable overlap

### 5.3.7 Solution Diagram

A solution diagram (Figure 55) was made in order to better understand connections and assumptions outlined above. In this model, the basis for water sector reform is community participation and education concerning urban planning. This would be facilitated by a coordinating body of some kind, operating under government mandate in cooperation with the water utility and private operators such as vendors. The assumption is that residents, if given the choice, would choose a combination of: expanded water utility network, public standpipes, and rainwater collection (for household and agriculture). According to

Clear, coordinated, long-term investment targets would be set to guide government policy, donors, and other financing. A financially sustainable water utility that generates enough revenues to cover investment requirements would be one result of this process.

Displaying the solution diagram in this way makes it clear that all factors are interdependent; pursuing one aspect and neglecting others will make it more difficult to achieve long term success.

It is important to note how investment priorities in this model (small scale/household supply, institutional planning/capacity) are quite different than current water sector investments in the region (mostly large central treatment plants to expand supply). This is not to say supply expansion (including desalination) is not justified; rather, that is must take place in a framework of community involvement and good sector management.

## 5.4 Priorities for Improving Affordability and Access

Significant investment is needed for water infrastructure in the Greater Accra Area. Even with planned expansion projects, at current growth rates, supply will not cover demand unless system capacity of 200,000-700,000 m3/day is added by 2030 (see Ch. 2 for details). However, this issue of utility supply does not address the fact that, if supply was covered, many residents are not served by the water utility, resulting in low accessibility to affordable water. This further complicates problems for the utility such as high commercial loss rates and low profit margins. Along with expanding supply, addressing this issue faced mainly by low-income residents is a priority.

Based on the problem analysis, it was determined that the water sector in Accra has lacked a pro-poor perspective and community involvement. The assumption that private sector management and foreign donor agencies would resolve the longstanding issues is symptomatic of this economic/political exclusion of local stakeholders. The problem analysis explains how this has led to higher water prices for the urban poor as well as more difficulties in their accessing water. The solution analysis identifies community-based approaches that could help resolve the situation given economic constraints.

Based on the above analysis, 10 investment priorities for improving affordable water supply to the majority of the population are as follows (author’s own list based on above research):

<!-- PDF page 145 -->

i. Coordinated policy direction/targets that integrate housing, water, and urban agriculture in the Greater Accra area;

ii. The creation of a coordinating body responsible for water services, education, and consultation in poor urban areas/informal settlements;

iii. A process to integrate and certify private water vendors through health, professional and operational standards;

iv. Creation of at least 1,000 public standpipes within 500m from residents households; connection to the utility and operated based on a pay per service fee, in cooperation with water vendors;

v. Creation of financing mechanisms for urban residents and farmers to make water-related investment;

vi. Distribution of at least 5,000 household rainwater capture systems and 200 pond capture systems.

vii. Utility connection once feasible.

viii. Reduction of physical water losses through metering and repair of distribution network

ix. Expansion of connections for newly built/existing urban settlements;

x. Further expansion of supply through new treatment plants and alternative supply sources such as desalination.

Many of these priorities are organizational in nature and could be achieved with an estimated $30-50 million or roughly the yearly investment amount in the urban water sector and only 5% of the total investment required to meet MDGs with respect to water in Ghana. Priorities vii-x, however, require more capital intensive investments. Precise costs and feasibility is a question for further study.

## 5.5 Industrial Water Consumers

The above analysis was done from the perspective of lower-income residential consumers. Priorities in terms of water investment for other consumers, such as industry, may be different.

Similar to residential consumers, due to supply disruptions, factories and businesses often must pay for water from private vendors at a much higher cost than the tariff rate. The opportunity cost for a business that must suspend/delay business operations in such a case, can be significant. For example, water shutdowns have led to layoff of workers and shut down of operations in the industrial zone of Tema leading to losses in the billions38 and inability for basic services (eg. health care) to be met. In September 2011, such a shutdown resulted from failure in the pipeline from Volta River (indicating the implications of overreliance on this source). At other times, water rationing and shortage is due to power blackouts (see Ch. 1 and 2).

In the Accra area, commercial/industrial water demand is estimated at 82,000m3/day or 23% of water supplied. The industrial water tariff is $0.93/m3 and the special commercial rate (for businesses that use water as a main part of their operation eg. bottling facilities) is $2.63/m3. The cost of production for desalination is well below the commercial and industrial rates. The advantage of having a stable, reliable water source in the country’s industrial and administrative centre (Accra) may justify a desalination facility, since other such supply options within the city are limited to surface water over 50km away (see Ch. 2). From the perspective of industrial and commercial consumers, priorities for water infrastructure investment would be different than domestic consumers, such as (in no particular order):

i.      Upgrades/expansion of electricity generation and distribution to
prevent supply shortages.

ii.     Upgrades/expansion of water distribution network to prevent rationing.

iii.    Water supply expansions to ensure demand is met 24h/day.

Points i. and iii. can be fulfilled with a power and water cogeneration facility as characterized in this study.
