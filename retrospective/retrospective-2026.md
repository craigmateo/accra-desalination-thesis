# Retrospective, 2026

## Introduction

This short retrospective revisits my 2013 master’s thesis, *Systems Analysis of Power and Water Cogeneration: A Case Study in Accra, Ghana*. The thesis examined water and energy infrastructure in the Greater Accra Metropolitan Area and used a proposed seawater desalination facility as the starting point for a broader systems analysis.

The purpose of returning to the project thirteen years later is not simply to ask whether the original analysis was “right” or “wrong.” Engineering studies necessarily simplify the systems they describe. They define boundaries, choose variables, make assumptions, and model relationships that can be expressed quantitatively. The more interesting retrospective question is what happened when those assumptions encountered the institutional, financial, technical, and social conditions of an actual infrastructure project.

One distinction is important from the outset. The plant eventually constructed at Teshie-Nungua was **not** the cogeneration system modeled in my thesis. My project examined a conceptual integrated water-energy system in which seawater reverse osmosis would be coupled with natural-gas combined-cycle power generation. The real plant was a stand-alone seawater reverse-osmosis facility. The comparison here is therefore not between my proposed design and a later implementation of that design. It is between the assumptions and priorities of the 2013 analysis and the subsequent history of the real desalination project.

That history is revealing. Some parts of the original technical analysis held up reasonably well. Other parts now look incomplete. Most importantly, the later history makes visible a variable that engineering models often treat as external: **institutional reliability**.

## 1. What the 2013 Thesis Argued

The thesis began from a problem larger than desalination. Accra faced simultaneous pressures on water supply and electricity generation, and I argued that the two should be considered together because water infrastructure consumes energy while power infrastructure also depends on water. The project therefore combined water-resource assessment, energy-resource assessment, technology selection, engineering-system modeling, cost estimation, and analysis of affordability and access.

The technical analysis did not conclude that desalination was the only or even the primary answer to Accra’s water needs. Lake Volta appeared likely to remain the most important potable-water source because of its enormous available volume and lower production cost. Desalination was instead treated as a potentially useful additional source: expensive relative to conventional freshwater, but attractive because seawater is effectively unlimited and because an additional source could diversify supply.

Within the desalination alternatives, reverse osmosis emerged as the preferred technology. The thesis also argued that, if a large desalination facility were built, there could be advantages in integrating it with natural-gas combined-cycle generation. The modeled system used a shared seawater intake and outfall, exploited the lower cost of electricity produced on site, and treated water and power as parts of one infrastructure system rather than as separate projects.

The modeled figures were ambitious. The conceptual power plant had a net output of about 1,426 MW, while the modeled reverse-osmosis plant had a production capacity of about 480,000 m³/day. The estimated production cost of desalinated water was approximately US$0.55/m³. These numbers were not forecasts of what Ghana would necessarily build. They were outputs of a systems model designed to test the technical and economic logic of integrated infrastructure at scale.

The thesis also contained a second argument that, in retrospect, matters at least as much as the engineering model. Chapter 5 examined affordability and access. It argued that increasing bulk water supply would not automatically make water affordable or accessible to low-income residents. Many households were not directly connected to the utility network and instead depended on vendors, tankers, standpipes, or sachet water. The thesis therefore distinguished **water production** from **water access**.

That distinction led to one of its central conclusions:

> Desalination is not the answer to the underlying issues in the urban water sector, which are largely organizational and social, rather than technical problems.

I would phrase parts of that argument differently today, but the distinction between producing more water and delivering reliable, affordable water remains central to this retrospective.

## 2. What Was Subsequently Built

The Teshie-Nungua desalination plant was inaugurated in April 2015, roughly two years after the thesis was submitted. It was designed to produce approximately 60,000 m³ of potable water per day—about 13 million gallons—and to supply communities including Teshie, Nungua, and surrounding parts of Accra. The project cost was reported at about US$126 million and was structured as a long-term build-own-operate-transfer arrangement.

Technically, the plant used seawater reverse osmosis. In that limited but important sense, the technology choice examined in the thesis was consistent with what was subsequently constructed. The real project, however, was much smaller than my conceptual system and was not coupled to a natural-gas combined-cycle plant.

The operational history was uneven. Ghana Water Company shut the plant down in January 2018 while contractual, technical, and financial issues were being addressed. It later returned to operation; by March 2020 it was reported to be producing about 40,000 m³/day, below its 60,000 m³/day design capacity. In 2022, residents in Teshie and Nungua were reported as describing water supply from the plant as relatively stable, illustrating that the facility could provide a meaningful service when operating.

The plant was shut down again in October 2025. Ghana Water Limited attributed the closure to unresolved contractual challenges and the absence of critical maintenance needed for safe and efficient operation. By early 2026, communities served by the plant were again reporting serious water shortages while negotiations continued over its future.

This is not a simple story of technical failure. The plant demonstrated that large-scale seawater reverse osmosis could operate in Accra and supply substantial quantities of potable water. At the same time, its history shows that technical capacity alone does not guarantee reliable infrastructure.

## 3. What Held Up

The first part of the original analysis that held up reasonably well was the basic technological judgment. Reverse osmosis was already becoming the dominant large-scale seawater desalination technology in 2013, and the Teshie-Nungua plant was built using that process. The thesis emphasized the falling energy demand of modern membrane desalination, the modularity of RO systems, and their advantages relative to thermal processes in circumstances where low-cost waste heat was not already available.

The second point that held up was the argument for diversification. When the desalination plant was operating, it provided an additional source for communities along Accra’s eastern coastal corridor. When the plant was unavailable, those communities experienced the consequences. The 2025–2026 shutdown therefore illustrates both sides of the security-of-supply argument: diversification can improve resilience, but a diversified source is useful only if that source itself is reliable.

The third point concerns affordability and access. The thesis repeatedly argued that constructing a new treatment plant would not by itself solve the problem of unequal access to water. That distinction still appears important. A treatment plant produces water at a particular point in an infrastructure network. Whether that water reaches households depends on distribution pipes, pumping, storage, utility finances, maintenance, legal arrangements, billing systems, and household connections.

Finally, the thesis was right to treat infrastructure as a system rather than as an isolated machine. Where the analysis now seems incomplete is not in taking a systems approach, but in where I drew the boundary of the system.

## 4. What the Original Analysis Underestimated

The largest weakness of the 2013 work is that its most detailed modeling concentrated on physical and economic variables that could be represented in engineering terms: mass flow, energy demand, pressure, recovery ratio, plant capacity, capital cost, and unit production cost.

Those variables matter. But a production-cost estimate is not the same thing as a viable long-term contract.

The later history of the Teshie-Nungua plant makes that distinction unusually clear. Reports surrounding the 2018 shutdown focused not only on technical questions but also on the financial terms under which Ghana Water purchased desalinated water. Public Utility Workers’ Union representatives argued that the cost to the utility was substantially higher than the regulated tariff at which water could be sold. Those claims became part of a broader renegotiation of the arrangement.

Whether every criticism of the original contract was justified is a separate question. The more general lesson is that the financial architecture of infrastructure can determine whether technically functional equipment remains operable. My thesis calculated the approximate production cost of water. It did not model, in comparable detail, the consequences of purchase agreements, currency exposure, payment obligations, allocation of maintenance responsibilities, or the bargaining relationship between a public utility and a private plant operator.

This is a significant difference between a model of a plant and a model of infrastructure.

In retrospect, I would also treat maintenance more explicitly. Engineering models often assume a maintenance regime and incorporate an annual O&M cost. But “maintenance” is not simply a number in a cost table. It is an organizational capability: spare parts have to be available, responsibilities have to be defined, contractors have to be paid, technical staff have to be retained, and interventions have to occur at the right time. The 2025 shutdown, which Ghana Water Limited linked directly to both contractual and maintenance problems, makes this point particularly visible.

## 5. Affordability and Access

Chapter 5 was the point at which the thesis began to move beyond conventional engineering analysis. The original research found that households without direct utility connections could pay considerably more for water than connected households because they depended on intermediary vendors, tankers, or packaged water. Increasing total system capacity could therefore coexist with severe household-level water insecurity.

The thesis proposed comparatively modest measures such as public standpipes, integration and regulation of small-scale vendors, rainwater harvesting, distribution-network improvement, and closer coordination between housing and water policy. Some of those specific recommendations would need to be reconsidered with current data. The larger analytical point remains important: **the unit of analysis matters**.

If the unit of analysis is the treatment plant, success can mean producing 60,000 m³/day.

If the unit of analysis is the utility, success may mean recovering costs, maintaining pressure, reducing non-revenue water, and keeping the network operating.

If the unit of analysis is the household, success means something more immediate: safe water arrives when needed and can be purchased without imposing an unreasonable financial or time burden.

Those measures of success overlap, but they are not identical.

The history of the desalination plant reinforces this point. When the facility was shut in late 2025, the problem did not remain confined to the plant boundary. Residents in the affected communities reported irregular supply and greater reliance on tankers and other private sources. A failure in one part of the infrastructure system was transferred into household costs, inconvenience, and uncertainty.

## 6. Institutional Reliability

The concept I would add most explicitly to the original thesis today is **institutional reliability**.

In engineering, reliability usually refers to the probability that a component or system will perform its intended function over time. Pumps, turbines, membranes, valves, electrical systems, and pipelines all have failure probabilities and maintenance requirements.

But infrastructure also depends on institutions that can fail.

A plant may be mechanically capable of producing water while remaining unavailable because a contract is disputed. A pump may be technically maintainable while remaining offline because responsibility for servicing it is unclear. A utility may have sufficient physical supply but lack the financial capacity to purchase that supply under existing contractual terms. A distribution network may exist but fail to reach particular households because of land tenure, planning, or connection costs.

These are not external political or administrative details added after the “real” engineering has been completed. They are part of the operating system of the infrastructure.

Seen this way, the Teshie-Nungua history suggests a broader definition of water security. Security of supply is not simply the existence of multiple physical water sources. It also requires that the institutional arrangements supporting those sources remain durable enough for the infrastructure to function.

That was the variable I did not model adequately in 2013.

## 7. Reflections on Engineering, Policy, and Infrastructure

Looking back, I can see this thesis as a transition point in my own work.

I began with a recognizably engineering question: what combination of water source, energy source, desalination technology, and plant configuration would provide the most effective infrastructure system? The thesis used multi-criteria analysis, thermodynamic modeling, membrane calculations, and cost estimation to answer that question.

But the project kept pushing outward.

Once I asked why people could lack affordable water in a city with large water infrastructure, the explanation could no longer be expressed entirely through engineering variables. It required attention to tariffs, utility organization, housing, informal markets, public policy, institutional history, and the way problems themselves are defined.

That movement eventually took me much further into the study of institutions, policy, and language. I would not now reject the technical work in the thesis in favour of a purely social explanation. The later history of the desalination plant shows why that would also be inadequate. Membrane performance matters. Energy consumption matters. Intake design matters. Maintenance matters. Production cost matters.

What changed is my understanding of where the engineering object ends.

In 2013, I was already trying to treat desalination as part of a larger water-energy system. Thirteen years later, I would draw that system boundary further outward.

The infrastructure is not only the desalination plant.

It is the plant, the power supply, the seawater intake, the membranes, the pipes, the reservoirs, the contracts, the tariffs, the maintenance regime, the utility, the financing arrangements, the regulators, the operators, and the households that ultimately depend on the water.

So the question I would now place at the centre of the project is no longer simply:

> **Can this technology work?**

It is:

> **What kind of system has to exist around the technology for it to solve the problem we actually care about?**

I think that is the question the 2013 thesis was beginning to ask, even if I did not yet have the language—or the evidence—to formulate it this way.
